Our history

Two kinds of teacher, one classroom

Rashtriya Vidhyapith began as a single-room pathshala under a peepal tree, where one teacher held class for whoever came. The building has grown since — laboratories, a library, a playing field — but the school still measures itself by that first classroom's standard: does the student understand, or have they only memorised?

We teach the full modern curriculum required of any school today, and alongside it, we keep alive the older methods this land taught with for centuries: recitation, dialogue, apprenticeship, and patience. Neither is treated as decoration for the other.

"A teacher who only fills a vessel has done half the work. The other half is teaching the student to ask why the vessel was empty."

— said to every new teacher at Rashtriya Vidhyapith, on their first day
What we study

A modern curriculum, taught by hand

Classes 1 through 8, following the national curriculum framework, with smaller-than-usual sections so that a teacher can still know every student by name.

Sciences & Mathematics

Science and mathematics through class 8, with a working laboratory for hands-on demonstrations, not just a textbook.

Sanskrit & Classical Languages

Compulsory Sanskrit through class 8, taught through recitation and grammar rather than translation alone.

Social Sciences

History, civics, geography and economics, taught with an emphasis on primary sources and local fieldwork.

Fine Arts

Painting and craft, taught through practice and observation rather than a fixed technique manual.

Computing & Digital Literacy

Programming fundamentals from class 6, and digital literacy woven into every subject, not taught apart from them.

How we teach

Five habits from the old Gurukul, still in use

None of these are museum pieces. Each one shows up somewhere in our timetable this week, adapted to a classroom of thirty rather than a courtyard of five.

1

Guru-Shishya Parampara — learning through relationship

Understanding was built through a long, direct relationship between one teacher and a few students, not a lecture to a hall.

THEN

A single teacher took full responsibility for a small group of students across many years.

NOW

Every student here is assigned a mentor-teacher who follows their progress across subjects, not just one.

2

Shruti — learning by careful listening

Long verses were carried for generations through exact spoken repetition, before any of it was written down.

THEN

Students repeated verses aloud after their teacher until pronunciation and rhythm were exact.

NOW

Sanskrit and poetry classes still open with recitation, before the text is ever opened on a page.

3

Gurukul life — study and daily work together

Students once lived with their teacher, and lessons were folded into the ordinary work of the household.

THEN

Cooking, gardening and cleaning were taught as seriously as grammar or arithmetic.

NOW

Every class keeps a rostered "duty period" — kitchen garden, library upkeep, junior-class tutoring.

4

Learning outdoors, under a tree

A courtyard or a tree's shade was the original classroom, chosen for quiet rather than walls.

THEN

Classes were held outdoors whenever weather allowed, seated in a loose circle around the teacher.

NOW

Our courtyard classroom under the old banyan is booked out most mornings, weather permitting.

5

Shastrartha — learning by argument

Understanding was tested in open, reasoned debate, where a student had to defend an idea in front of others.

THEN

Scholars debated a text's meaning point by point, in front of an audience that judged the reasoning.

NOW

Every class holds a weekly debate period on a question from that week's lesson, no notes allowed.
